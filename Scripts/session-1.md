### Slide 9

A quick word about language. You'll hear us say Autistic child or Autistic person. Many Autistic people prefer this identity-first language because autism is part of who they are. Some prefer person on the autism spectrum, or another description. Each person's preference matters.

Follow your child's lead wherever possible, and be open to their preferences changing. You may still be finding words that feel comfortable in your family. There's no test on terminology today. Our purpose is to speak respectfully and to listen to how people describe themselves.

### Slide 14

Neurodiversity means the diversity of human brains and minds. It includes everyone. Neurotypical and neurodivergent describe patterns within that wider picture, and autism is one expression of it.

These words help us talk about differences in experience and processing. They don't tell us everything about a particular person. As we explore common patterns today, keep asking how they do, or don't, connect with the child you know. Each child remains an individual.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 1, slide 14; matching slide PDF page 14; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 20; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 15

A neurodiversity-affirming approach recognises different ways of knowing, doing, being and belonging. People think and learn in different ways. Everyone has strengths, and everyone can experience challenges.

Take the things you wrote about your child earlier. Their interests, enjoyment and way of connecting are part of the picture when we think about support. We can take difficulties seriously while valuing who they are. That means asking what the surroundings, routines and support need to look like so your child can participate comfortably and authentically. A hard day doesn't cancel out a strength, and recognising a strength doesn't make a hard day disappear.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 1, slide 15; matching slide PDF page 15; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 21; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 16

Autism is one part of a person's identity. Culture, language, gender, age, disability, faith and family circumstances can overlap and shape opportunities, barriers and experiences. That's the idea of intersectionality.

The words on this slide are prompts to widen our understanding. You don't need to share personal identity information here. For your child, useful support takes account of the whole person and the family context. We can stay curious about parts of their experience we haven't yet understood.

### Slide 17

Capacity can change, sometimes within the same day. Something your child managed on a quiet Saturday might be much harder after a full Monday at school. The child is the same, but the demands, sensory load and available energy may be different.

The phrase dynamic disability helps us keep that changing experience in view. When something becomes difficult, ask what else is going on and what support fits now. Yesterday's capacity doesn't tell us exactly what is available today. We'll come back to this in more detail when we talk about daily energy.

### Slide 18

Let's hold on to joy and flourishing as we move forward. Connection, belonging and small moments of enjoyment matter. Every family is different, and understanding what helps can take time.

Doing something differently is fine when it helps your family. A quieter outing, a familiar shared activity or a small success can matter just as much as a bigger milestone. In our next session, we'll begin with how the world arrives through the senses, and then explore attention and thinking. Keep your child's strengths alongside your questions.
