### Slide 3

Sensory processing is the way the brain receives, processes and responds to information from the senses. We often notice the response first, but there is a whole process happening before that response becomes visible.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 2, slide 3; matching slide PDF page 3; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 28; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 4

Many of us learnt five senses: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. Here we include three more. Proprioception is our sense of the body in space, drawing on information from muscles and joints. It helps us know where our body is and how much force we're using.

The vestibular sense relates to balance and movement. Interoception is our sense of internal body signals, such as hunger, thirst or needing the toilet. We'll return to that later. These senses work together. In a busy routine, someone may be managing several kinds of sensory information at once, including input that others barely notice.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 2, slide 4; matching slide PDF page 4; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 29; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 5

Think about taking off a jumper because you're getting hot. First, information arrives from the body. The brain processes it, including what similar experiences have felt like before. Then there is a response, such as taking the jumper off.

This process helps us understand our surroundings, keep track of our body and engage with what's happening. People process sensory information differently. A visible reaction is just one part of that process. Before judging the reaction, it helps to ask what information may have arrived, and how it might have felt.

### Slide 8

This framework from Winnie Dunn describes four patterns of sensory response. Registration means some input may not be picked up readily. Sensory seeking means actively looking for more input, perhaps through movement, touch or sound. Sensory sensitivity means noticing input strongly, even if the person doesn't act to change it. Sensory avoiding means actively reducing input, such as covering ears or choosing a quieter place.

A person may seek one kind of input and avoid another. The pattern can also shift with the setting and the load already being carried. Some families will have encountered a sensory profile through an occupational therapist. Use this as a way to notice patterns and ask questions. It doesn't give us a complete description of a child.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 2, slide 8; matching slide PDF page 8; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 33; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 9

Stimming means repetitive movement or sound, such as rocking, flapping, spinning, humming or tapping. It can help a child settle, focus, cope or express joy. You may recognise small versions in yourself, such as jiggling your leg.

Notice what stimming seems to do for your child. Make room for it at home, and help others understand its purpose. If something causes harm, the question becomes how to meet the same need more safely. Simply taking it away can remove something that was helping. Some children hold their stims back at school. Home can offer room for authentic expression without that extra effort.

### Slide 12

The detail-first idea describes building understanding from individual pieces towards a whole picture. Someone may notice a small change, an inconsistency or a background detail before the overall situation becomes clear.

This can be connected with strengths such as pattern recognition, deep memory, logical precision, honesty and close attention. Think about what your child knows in detail, or what they notice that other people miss. These strengths are present in everyday life.

Taking in a lot of detail can also take effort. A task may be hard to begin if the larger picture or first step is unclear. The same way of processing can bring depth and also create demands. We can notice both without turning this into a checklist that every child must fit.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 2, slide 12; matching slide PDF page 12; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 37; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 13

Think about your own attention for a moment. Does it move between several things, or become deeply absorbed in one? The torch and floodlight image gives us two ways to picture that. Monotropism describes attention going deeply into fewer things at a time. Autistic people help develop this way of understanding attention.

Deep interests can offer learning, memory and joy. When your child is absorbed in building, reading about space or listening to a favourite song, their attention may be deeply invested. Asking them to stop for dinner can involve real effort. Warning, time and a helpful transition can make a difference. That absorbed state, sometimes called flow, is also something worth protecting.

### Slide 14

Executive functioning is a name for processes we use to organise and carry out everyday tasks. The slide includes planning, organisation, time management and working memory. It also includes thinking about our thinking, managing emotions, flexibility, attention, getting started and persisting.

We use different combinations of these throughout the day. When a task is hard, identifying the particular demand can help us find useful support. Let's look at getting started and switching focus, because they ask different things of a person.

### Slide 15

Getting ready for school can sound like one instruction, but it contains many steps. Where do I start? What comes next? What needs to be ready at the end? If the whole task isn't clear, the first step may be difficult to find.

A visible sequence or showing the first step can help make the task more understandable. Before adding another reminder, ask which part needs to become clearer.

### Slide 16

Switching focus is a different demand. A child deeply absorbed in play may need time to disengage before they can take up the next activity. An unexpected interruption can feel much bigger than it looks from the outside.

Think back to the torch beam. A warning that means what it says, time to finish a small part, and a clear sense of what comes next can support a transition. Check what works for your child.

### Slide 19

Let's return to interoception, the body's inside sense that we named earlier. Internal signals add another part to the picture. Some experiences are hard to notice or describe even when they strongly affect how a person feels.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 2, slide 19; matching slide PDF page 19; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 44; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 20

Interoception concerns signals from inside the body: temperature, hunger, thirst or needing the bathroom. Alexithymia describes difficulty identifying and describing emotions and feelings. They're related ideas, but noticing a body signal and knowing how to describe an emotion are different tasks.

A child might notice hunger only when it becomes intense, or find it hard to tell whether a fluttery feeling before a party is excitement or worry. These examples give us questions to explore, rather than a diagnosis. We can offer time and ways to communicate what is happening inside, without insisting on an immediate answer.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Primary, Session 2, slide 20; matching slide PDF page 20; PC Primary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 45; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 21

Sometimes a build-up is happening inside even when the surface doesn't show it. Early signs may be subtle, individual or simply unavailable to us. This is an invitation to learn together, never a reason to blame yourself for not noticing sooner.

Over time, your child and the people who know them may build a shared picture of what helps. Describing changes clearly can help family members and school respond more consistently. The picture should stay open to what your child communicates and to things we haven't understood yet.
